Every year I print a box of pictures no bigger than a paperback and give it to the people in them. It is the least modern thing I do and the most useful. This is a note on why, and on the strange things a small print teaches you about a photograph you thought you knew.

The screen lies a little

A picture on a screen is lit from behind and it flatters everything: the shadows glow, the blacks are never quite black, and a dull frame looks moody. Paper is honest. A print lit by the window shows you what you actually made, and about a third of the time it is not what you thought.

Why small

A big print is an argument. A small one is a conversation. Held in the hand, a picture the size of a postcard asks to be looked at closely, then passed to someone else, then put in a book and found again in ten years. Nobody has ever found a poster in a book.

What fits in a box

  • Forty prints, no more. Choosing is most of the work.
  • One size, so nothing shouts.
  • No captions. If a picture needs one, it stays out.
  • A line on the lid with the year and the place.

What a print teaches

Edges, first. On a screen you look at the middle; on paper the eye goes to the corners, and the corners are where the mistakes live: a pole, a stray hand, a bright patch that pulls the eye off the face. Then tone. A print has less room than a screen, so the picture has to earn its blacks. And finally sequence. Forty prints on a table arrange themselves into an order the screen never suggested.

Print it small and the photograph tells you what it is. Print it big and it tells you what you wanted it to be.

How to start

Pick ten pictures from the last year, order them at postcard size from anywhere decent, and put them on a table by a window. Leave them there for a week. Move them around when you pass. By Friday you will know which three are photographs and which seven were only files.
